Most students revise mathematics the way they revise history: re-reading notes and hoping the ideas stick. Mathematics does not work like that. It is a skill, closer to playing an instrument than memorising a speech. You get better by doing, not by reading about doing. This guide gives you a revision method that actually matches how the subject works.

## The re-reading trap

Re-reading a chapter feels productive because the material looks familiar. That feeling is fluency, not knowledge. In an exam, nobody asks you to recognise a method — you have to produce it from a blank page. So the golden rule of maths revision is simple: **if you are not writing, you are not revising.**

## The loop: recall, work, mix

Revise each topic in a tight loop of three steps.

**1. Recall first.** Close your notes and write down everything you remember about the topic — definitions, methods, formulas. This will feel uncomfortable. That discomfort is the point: pulling an idea out of memory is what strengthens it. If you cannot recall the steps for [solving linear equations](/subjects/mathematics/cambridge/igcse/algebra/linear-equations), you have found your starting point.

**2. Work examples actively.** Open the lesson and work through one example *before* looking at the solution. Cover the working, try it yourself, then compare. Pay attention to where your attempt diverged — that gap is exactly what you need to practise. Do the same with [expressions](/subjects/mathematics/cambridge/igcse/algebra/expressions): simplify a few, expand a few, and check each line.

**3. Mix it up.** Once a topic feels solid on its own, practise it alongside other topics. Exams never label questions by chapter, so your revision should not either. Alternate between [linear equations](/subjects/mathematics/cambridge/igcse/algebra/linear-equations) and [quadratic equations](/subjects/mathematics/cambridge/igcse/algebra/quadratic-equations) in the same session. It feels harder — that is why it works.

## Build a mistake log

Keep a single page per subject where you write down every error you make during practice, in your own words: "I forgot to flip the inequality sign when dividing by a negative" is worth more than ten correct answers. Before each revision session, re-do two problems from the log. Errors you never revisit are errors you will repeat in the exam.

## How much, how often

Short sessions beat marathons. Forty focused minutes a day, spread over weeks, will take you further than a single eight-hour weekend. Spacing works because forgetting is part of learning — each time you reconstruct a method you had started to forget, the memory gets stronger.

## A sample week

- **Monday:** Recall and practise one chapter ([Algebra](/subjects/mathematics/cambridge/igcse/algebra) — expressions and equations).
- **Wednesday:** Mixed practice — alternate algebra questions with an older topic.
- **Friday:** Timed past-paper questions; log every mistake.
- **Sunday:** Re-do the mistake log, then preview next week's chapter.

Revision is not about finding the perfect technique. It is about doing real problems, regularly, and paying attention to where you go wrong. Start today with one topic, one blank page, and one worked example — then repeat.
